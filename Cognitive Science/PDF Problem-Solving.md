## 1. Cognition and Problem-Solving

**Cognition:** Mental activities involved in thinking, knowing, remembering, and communicating. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving

**Problem-solving:** The cognitive process of overcoming obstacles to reach a goal when the solution is not immediately obvious.

**Reasoning vs. Problem-solving**

- Reasoning: Applying logic to reach a conclusion.
- Problem-solving: Overcoming obstacles to achieve a goal, often using domain-specific knowledge.

## 2. Well-Defined vs. Ill-Defined Problems

|Well-defined|Ill-defined|
|---|---|
|Clear initial state|Initial state may be unclear|
|Clear goal|Goal may be ambiguous|
|Established rules and operations|Rules or methods may be unclear|
|Solution can be checked against clear criteria|Many possible acceptable solutions|
|Examples: Sudoku, mathematical equations|Examples: Writing a novel, improving college life|

**Remember:** The main difference is the clarity of the problem, goal, and rules.

## 3. Four Fundamental Terms

|Term|Meaning|Example: Maze|
|---|---|---|
|Initial state|Starting situation|Entrance|
|Goal state|Desired outcome|Exit|
|Operators|Actions that change the state|Permitted movements|
|Problem space|Possible states and transitions|Possible paths through the maze|

These concepts form the foundation of the problem-solving model. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving

## 4. Steps in Problem-Solving

Memorize the sequence:

**Identify → Represent → Choose strategy → Apply operators → Evaluate → Revise if necessary**

- Identify: Recognize the problem.
- Represent: Form a mental model of the problem.
- Choose strategy: Select an appropriate approach.
- Apply operators: Perform the actions.
- Evaluate: Check whether the goal is achieved.
- Revise: Try another approach if necessary.

## 5. Concepts, Prototypes, and Concept Shifting

**Concept:** A mental category grouping similar objects, events, ideas, or people.

**Prototype:** The most typical example of a concept in your mind.

Example: Concept = Bird; Prototype = Sparrow.

**Category shifting:** According to the slides, a remembered item may gradually seem more similar to the mental prototype than it actually was.

**Concept shifting:** Changing your understanding or classification of a category based on new information.

Example: Initially believing all birds can fly, then learning about penguins and ostriches and revising that belief.

### Advantages and disadvantages of concepts

**Advantages:** Speed, efficiency, quick decision-making.

**Disadvantages:** Stereotyping, errors in judgment, miscommunication. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving

## 6. Novices vs. Experts

|Novices|Experts|
|---|---|
|Limited, often surface-level knowledge|Deep, organized knowledge|
|Trial and error|Schemas and heuristics|
|Focus on surface features|Focus on deep structure|
|Slower, more effortful|Faster, more efficient|

**Schema:** An organized mental structure of knowledge.

**Heuristic:** A mental shortcut or practical strategy used to solve problems quickly.

## 7. Newell and Simon's Problem-Solving Flow

The slides describe the model associated with **Newell and Simon (1972)**.

Memorize this sequence:

1. Read the problem.
2. Encode it into working memory.
3. Search long-term memory (LTM) for algorithms and heuristics.
4. Execute the chosen strategy.
5. Compare the new state with the goal.
6. If there is a mismatch, return to earlier steps.
7. If the goal is achieved, stop.

**Working memory:** Holds and manipulates the information currently being used.

**Long-term memory:** Stores knowledge, experiences, and strategies for later use. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving

## 8. Heuristics and Problem-Solving Strategies

**Heuristic:** An efficient mental shortcut that helps solve problems quickly. It does not guarantee the optimal solution.

### Three important strategies

|Strategy|Main idea|
|---|---|
|**Means-end analysis**|Reduce the difference between the current state and the goal.|
|**Anti-looping heuristic**|Avoid repetitive or unproductive moves.|
|**Subgoal structure**|Divide a large problem into smaller goals.|

**Example of means-end analysis:** To write a research paper, first select a topic, then find sources, organize the research, and write the paper.

**Example of anti-looping:** Avoid repeatedly exploring the same unproductive paths in a maze.

**Example of subgoals:** To run a marathon, progressively train for shorter distances before attempting the full distance. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving

### Algorithm vs. Heuristic

- **Algorithm:** A defined procedure that produces a guaranteed result when its requirements are satisfied.
- **Heuristic:** A shortcut that may save time but does not guarantee the best solution.

## 9. Real-World Problems

Real-world problems are complex and often ill-defined.

**Characteristics:**

- Initial state or goal may be unclear.
- Rules may be flexible or unknown.
- Multiple solutions may exist.
- Feedback may be delayed or ambiguous.
- Several social, emotional, and economic factors may interact.

**Examples:** Budget planning, managing a team, medical diagnosis, urban planning.

**Cognitive demands:** Flexible thinking, problem representation, decision-making under uncertainty, emotional regulation, and long-term planning. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving

## 10. General Problem Solver (GPS)

**General Problem Solver (GPS):** A problem-solving model associated with Newell and Simon (1972). It represents problem-solving as navigating through a problem space using operators and strategies to move from the initial state to the goal state.

### Main components

- Initial state
- Goal state
- Operators
- Problem space
- Strategies, such as means-end analysis

### Working Forward vs. Working Backward

|Working forward|Working backward|
|---|---|
|Starts from the initial state|Starts by considering the goal|
|Determines the next action|Determines what must happen before the goal|
|Progresses toward the goal|Traces the required steps back toward the start|

## 11. Tower of Hanoi

**Problem:** Move a stack of disks from the first peg to the third peg using the second peg as an auxiliary peg.

### Rules

1. Move only one disk at a time.
2. Move only the top disk of a peg.
3. Never place a larger disk on a smaller disk.

### Connection to GPS

|Component|Tower of Hanoi|
|---|---|
|Initial state|All disks on the first peg|
|Goal state|All disks on the third peg|
|Operators|Legal disk movements|
|Constraints|Follow the three rules|
|Strategies|Means-end analysis, subgoals, anti-looping|

**Important:** Tower of Hanoi demonstrates how a problem can be broken into smaller subgoals and solved through valid, carefully sequenced actions. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving

## 12. Nine-Dot Problem

**Problem:** Connect nine dots arranged in a 3 × 3 grid using four or fewer straight lines without lifting your pen.

**Key insight:** Lines can extend beyond the boundaries of the grid.

**Connection to GPS:**

- Initial state: Nine unconnected dots.
- Goal state: All nine dots connected.
- Operators: Drawing straight lines.
- Constraints: Do not lift the pen; use at most four lines.
- Problem space: Possible line sequences and directions.

**Cognitive science lesson:** We sometimes impose restrictions that are not part of a problem. Reframing the problem can reveal a solution. Notes_Sept9_10_ProblemSolving
